January the eleventh. French and Belgian troops march into the Ruhr, the heart of German heavy industry, because Germany is behind on deliveries of timber and coal.
Berlin calls for passive resistance, and pays the workers who down tools. It pays them by cashing government bills at the central bank. In other words, with new notes.
The dollar averages eighteen thousand marks in January. A hundred and ten thousand in June. Three hundred and fifty thousand in July.
Here is what that does to payday. Firms go from paying weekly, to paying daily, and then to paying in the lunch break.
Not everyone. Plenty of people are still paid by the week. But that's the direction.
At some factory gates, workers collect their wages with handcarts. The notes are handed out by the sack.
You've heard the story of the thief who stole the basket and left the money. A British newspaper printed it early this year, as a story going round Berlin. It was a joke before it was a legend.
Once you're paid, you run. A statistician worked out that being paid one day later than your neighbour could cost you a fifth of what your wages would buy.
A woman who was nine that year remembered her father, a teacher, being paid every day. He came home at noon with a briefcase full of notes, and her mother ran straight to the grocer.
And often the grocer had just pulled down his shutters. Lunch break. He used it to change the price tags.
Another Berliner remembered his father, a senior civil servant, who was still paid once a month.
So on payday: a monthly ticket for the underground, cheques for the rent and the school fees, and the whole family to the barber.
Next morning they're up at four or five and off to the wholesale market by taxi. Within an hour the month's salary is gone. Giant cheeses. Whole hams. Potatoes by the hundredweight.
Then no more money for a month. That isn't panic. That's the correct move.

Quest two. Find something that holds its value.
Not the mark. In August the president of the central bank tells a council that the bank is issuing twenty trillion marks of new money a day. Next week, he says, forty-six trillion.
By autumn, up to a hundred and thirty-three outside firms, with one thousand seven hundred and eighty-three presses, are printing day and night. Thirty paper mills make the paper.
Many notes are printed on one side only. In October a photo agency shows what people do with the blank back of a one-million-mark note. They write on it. A new notepad would cost billions.
Nobody says the mark is falling. A woman from Hamburg remembered what they said instead: the dollar is going up again.
In Berlin the artist Käthe Kollwitz writes down what a child on the tram asked, looking up. Mother, is that the dollar?
If you had real dollars, you were rich. The September before, a young reporter called Ernest Hemingway crossed from France into the German town of Kehl and changed ten francs. About ninety cents.
It paid for a day of heavy spending for him and his wife, with change left over. A five-course hotel dinner was fifteen cents.
A pastry shop, he wrote, was swept clean in half an hour.
You don't have dollars. So you do what the German Historical Museum says everyone tried to do: turn cash into things, as fast as possible.
Tradesmen start asking for food and coal instead of money. A photograph from this year shows a shop offering sales and repairs in exchange for food.
Towns and companies print their own emergency money. More than five thousand eight hundred of them.
And some of it isn't in marks at all. There are notes made out in rye. In coal. In sugar. In bacon.
What about shares? At the low point, in the autumn of nineteen twenty-two, the stock exchange valued the whole of Daimler at the price of three hundred and twenty-seven of its own cars.
One thing does get cheap. Rent. The law pegs it to what it was in July nineteen fourteen. Good news for you. Not for your landlord.
October. Prices are doubling every three point seven days. That is the measured peak.
In Berlin a printer's wife, Betty Scholem, writes to her son. A loaf is five hundred and forty million today. Tomorrow, she's sure, double.

Quest three: failed. It was never winnable.
Unless you read that last number from the other side.
Somebody owed those hundred and fifty-four billion marks. The state. And the state has just paid them off for fifteen pfennigs.
That's the hidden quest. Four: owe money.
A historian of Bavaria lists the winners. All the borrowers. The state, the entrepreneurs, the farmers.
A loan taken out in solid pre-war marks could be paid back in nineteen twenty-three with the same number of worthless ones.
Farmers shed mortgages that had sat on the land for generations.
One story, from the year before: a landowner in Pomerania borrows four million marks to buy the estate next door. That autumn he pays it all back by selling less than half the potatoes from one field.
Businessmen learn the rule. The same economist wrote it down: contract as many debts as possible.
The master of it is Hugo Stinnes. His group swells to one thousand five hundred and thirty-five companies.
He dies in April nineteen twenty-four, and the empire soon starts to fall apart.
Even here the win isn't clean. City landlords shed their mortgages too, and lose nearly all their rent.
But add it up, and one verdict from nineteen thirty stands: immense sums moved from creditor to debtor. And you, with your savings book, were a creditor.

On the fifteenth, two things happen. The central bank stops cashing the government's bills. No more printing for the state.
And a new bank starts handing out a new money. The Rentenmark. Small, ugly, grey-green notes, one Berliner remembered.
On paper it's backed by a mortgage on Germany's farms and factories. What matters is the hard limit on how much can be issued.
On the twentieth the rate is fixed. One dollar: four point two trillion marks. One Rentenmark: one trillion.
