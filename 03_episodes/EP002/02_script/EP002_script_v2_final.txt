It's September, nineteen twenty-nine. You have one thousand dollars.
That's about eight months' pay. It took you four years to save.
It can vanish in two days. It can shrink to a hundred and ten dollars. Or it can quietly buy more than it does today.
The difference is where you put it this week.
So let's put it in four places at once, and see which you survives.
You're twenty-four. You're a clerk in New York City.
You are made up. The numbers aren't.
Here's your quest log. One: pick a home for your thousand dollars.
Two: get through October.
Three: still have it in nineteen thirty-three.
There's a fourth quest hidden at the end. It's the reason you've never had to think about any of this.
First, the mood. The stock market has been climbing for eight years.
In nineteen twenty-one, the Dow Jones average stood at sixty-three. This month it touches three hundred and eighty-one.
Six times higher. Everyone you know has a story about a guy who got rich.
Almost nobody you know is that guy. Only about one and a half million Americans own any stock at all.
But the ones who do are loud.
So. Four doors.
Door one. The broker. You buy stocks on margin.
Margin means you put down a slice of the price, and the broker lends you the rest.
That slice can be as little as ten percent.
So your thousand dollars buys ten thousand dollars of stock.
If prices rise ten percent, you've doubled your money. This is the part the loud guys talk about.
The stock everybody wants is Radio. That's R C A.
In March, a pool of insiders pushed Radio up by almost half in ten days. Then they sold everything. That was legal.
Door two. Same broker, no loan. A thousand dollars of stock, bought and paid for.
Door three. The bank on the corner. Marble columns, brass rails, and it pays you interest.
Door four. The mattress. No interest, no risk, no respect.
Your coworkers think door four is for people who are afraid of money.
So we'll make four of you, and send one through each door.
Quest one, complete.
September third. The Dow closes at three eighty-one. Nobody rings a bell at the top.
Through September, prices slide about ten percent. Then they bounce.
In mid-October, a famous Yale economist says stock prices have reached what looks like a permanently high plateau.
He is about to become famous for a different reason.
Thursday, October twenty-fourth. Nearly thirteen million shares change hands. A record.
The big bankers step in and buy shares in public, to show everyone it's fine.
It holds for a weekend.
Monday, October twenty-eighth. The Dow falls nearly thirteen percent. In one day.
Now look at margin you. You owned ten thousand dollars of stock, with one thousand of your own.
A thirteen percent drop is thirteen hundred dollars. Your thousand is gone. So is three hundred you never had.
The broker doesn't wait for you to feel better. He sends a margin call. Pay up today, or he sells your shares.
You can't pay. He sells. So does every other broker in the city, at the same moment, into the same falling market.
That's Tuesday, October twenty-ninth. Sixteen million shares. Down nearly twelve percent more.
Margin you is finished. Out in two days.
To be fair, by nineteen twenty-nine many brokers wanted a lot more than ten percent down. Those customers lasted a little longer.
Stock you is bruised, but alive. No loan, no call. You still own every share.
By mid-November the market is down almost half. Your thousand is about five hundred.
Bank you and mattress you read about it in the paper, and feel very clever.
Quest two, complete. Three of you left.
Here's what nobody tells stock you. The crash isn't the bad part. The bad part is the next three years.
The market doesn't fall off a cliff and stop. It falls down a staircase.
Every rally looks like the bottom. None of them is.
The real bottom comes on July eighth, nineteen thirty-two. The Dow closes at forty-one.
That's eighty-nine percent below the peak.
Your thousand dollars of stock is now worth about a hundred and ten.
You didn't borrow. You didn't panic. You did everything right, and you kept eleven cents on the dollar.
Now, bank you. Still feeling clever?
A bank doesn't keep your money in a box with your name on it. It lends it out.
That works, as long as everyone doesn't ask for their money on the same morning.
In nineteen thirty, thirteen hundred and fifty banks shut their doors.
One of them is in your city. It's called the Bank of United States. Despite the name, it's an ordinary bank.
Two hundred million dollars in deposits. The biggest bank failure the country has ever seen.
Then you hear a rumor about your own bank. You go down at lunch. There's already a line around the block.
This is the trap. If you wait calmly, you might lose everything. If you run, you help break the bank.
And there's no insurance. If the bank dies, you join a different line, for whatever is left.
Nineteen thirty-one. About twenty-three hundred more banks go under.
By nineteen thirty-three, the count passes nine thousand.
Depositors lose about one point three billion dollars.
So bank you is a gamble, on a bank you can't see inside.
And mattress you? The coward?
Something strange is happening to the cash under the bed.
Prices are falling. Not stock prices. All prices. Rent. Shoes. Bread.
Between nineteen thirty and nineteen thirty-three, average prices drop by nearly thirty percent.
So a dollar buys more every month. Your thousand dollars now shops like fourteen hundred.
You took no risk, earned no interest, and came out ahead of everyone.
There is a catch. By nineteen thirty-three, one worker in four has no job.
If you're one of them, the mattress isn't a strategy. It's groceries.
And there was a fifth door that nobody at your office mentioned. The post office.
The Post Office takes savings. Two percent interest, run by the government, up to twenty-five hundred dollars.
By nineteen thirty-three, five times as many people are using it as before.
Quest three, complete. Scoreboard.
Margin you: zero, in two days.
Stock you: about a hundred and ten dollars.
Bank you: somewhere between a thousand and nothing, depending on a rumor.
Mattress you: a thousand dollars that buys like fourteen hundred.
Post office you: a thousand, plus interest.
Now the hidden one.
March, nineteen thirty-three. The banking system has collapsed. The new president closes every bank in the country.
They call it a bank holiday. Nobody is on holiday.
That June, he signs a law that creates something new. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. The F D I C.
From January first, nineteen thirty-four, if your bank fails, you get your money back. Up to twenty-five hundred dollars.
Your thousand is covered. All of it.
Think about what that does to the line around the block.
If your money is safe either way, there's no reason to run. And if nobody runs, the bank doesn't break.
The rumor stops working.
That's the fourth quest. Trust a bank again. And you didn't complete it. The rules did.
As for stock you. Say you held those shares and never looked.
The Dow gets back to its nineteen twenty-nine high on November twenty-third, nineteen fifty-four.
Twenty-five years. You were twenty-four. Now you're forty-nine.
That's counting price alone, not dividends. It's still a long time to be right.
So what was the smart move in September, nineteen twenty-nine?
With hindsight, the boring one. And nobody at the time could have proved it.
The economist had a plateau. The bankers had a plan. The guy at the office had a tip.
You had a thousand dollars and four doors.
Next time you walk into an American bank, look for a small sign by the door. F D I C.
It's there because of the line around the block.
Quest complete.
