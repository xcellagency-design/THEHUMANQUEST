[S1 COLD OPEN]
You were told slaves built the pyramids. Whips. Chains. A hundred thousand prisoners in the sand.
That picture comes from a Greek writer who showed up two thousand years late.
The people who actually built it left a diary, a payroll, and their own skeletons.
And their story is stranger. They were paid in beer. They couldn't say no. And one crew called itself the Drunkards.
So let's make you one of them.

[S2 PREMISE]
It's about 2560 BC. You're seventeen. You farm barley in a village on the Nile.
You are made up. Everything that happens to you is in the evidence.
Here's your quest log. One: get to Giza. Two: earn your bread. Three: get home in one piece.
There's a fourth quest hidden at the end. Almost nobody sees it coming.

[S3 QUEST ONE: THE DRAFT]
Every summer the Nile floods your fields. For weeks, there's nothing to farm.
That's when the king's men arrive. They don't want your grain. They want you.
Historians still argue about how much choice you had.
Zahi Hawass, Egypt's best-known archaeologist, calls men like you conscripts.
Mark Lehner, who dug up your future home, calls it obligatory labor. A tax you pay with your back.
He compares it to a barn-raising. Everyone is expected to show up. Not everyone is thrilled.
So you're not a slave. Nobody owns you. But "no" isn't on the menu either.
You climb into a boat with the other boys from your village, and you sail for Giza.
Quest one, complete.

[S4 THE CITY]
You expect a camp. You get a city.
Archaeologists call it the Lost City of the Pyramid Builders. The crown built it for exactly one job.
There are royal grain silos. There are bakeries by the dozen.
You sleep in a long hall, shoulder to shoulder, with forty or fifty other men.
About twenty thousand people may live here, counting the bakers, the brewers and the scribes.
Not a hundred thousand. And only about half of them ever touch a stone.
Then you get your team. Crews had names, and they painted them on the blocks.
One was the Friends of Khufu. A few kings later, another was the Drunkards of Menkaure.
Picture a company softball team. Except the season lasts for months, and the ball weighs two and a half tons.

[S5 QUEST TWO: THE JOB]
Here's the job. The Great Pyramid needs about 2.3 million blocks.
If the work took around twenty-three years, that's roughly 270 blocks a day.
One block every two or three minutes. All day. For two decades.
A typical block weighs about as much as a pickup truck.
When it's finished it will stand 146 metres tall. Nothing humans build will beat it for nearly four thousand years.
And you have rope, a wooden sledge, and your crew.
Plus one trick. Somebody walks in front and pours water on the sand.
It looks like a ritual. It's physics.
In 2014, physicists in Amsterdam tested it. Damp sand is about twice as stiff as dry sand.
The sledge stops digging in, and the force you need drops by half.
A later Egyptian tomb painting shows exactly that. A man at the front of a sledge, pouring water.
Half the effort. Still a truck.

[S6 THE DIARY]
The best stone doesn't come from here. The white outer blocks come from Tura, across the river.
We know because one of your coworkers kept a logbook.
His name was Merer. He ran a boat crew of about forty men.
In 2013 his diary turned up at an ancient harbour on the Red Sea. It's the oldest inscribed papyrus ever found.
And it is gloriously boring. Load stone. Sail to the pyramid. Sleep there. Sail back. Repeat.
Two or three round trips every ten days. About two hundred blocks a month.
He never once says the pyramid is amazing. He calls it by its name, Horizon of Khufu, and goes back for more rocks.
It's a timesheet. It just happens to be four and a half thousand years old.

[S7 PAYDAY]
So what does all this pay? Not coins. Coins won't exist for almost two thousand years.
You're paid in food. The standard ration for a laborer in this era was ten loaves of bread and a measure of beer. Per day.
Beer here is a staple food, not a party.
Bread moulds are the most common pottery in the whole city. Beer jars come second.
And there's meat. Hawass estimates twenty-one cattle and twenty-three sheep arrived every single day.
Lehner thinks most of it was served at big feasts.
Either way, you're eating beef. For a farm kid, that's a feast.
Quest two, complete. You earned your bread. Literally.

[S8 QUEST THREE: THE BODY]
Quest three is the hard one. Get home in one piece.
Because the builders' bones were found too. And bones keep receipts.
Worn-down spines. Worn-out joints. Arms and legs that had snapped.
But here's the twist. Most of those breaks had healed straight.
Somebody set them with a splint.
A few skeletons even show amputations the patient survived.
So when a block rolls onto your leg, and one day something will, you aren't left in the sand.
Someone carries you out. Someone sets the bone. And you keep getting your bread.
Four and a half thousand years ago, you had workplace healthcare.
It still wasn't a long life. Most of the people in that cemetery died in their thirties.
But if your months are up and your legs still work, you sail home. A new crew takes your bunk.
Quest three, complete. Barely.

[S9 HIDDEN QUEST]
Now the hidden one.
The pyramid is a machine for one man's afterlife. Yours isn't in the budget.
But in 1990, a cemetery turned up in the shadow of the pyramids. Small, modest tombs. The builders' own.
Not every grave belongs to Khufu's crews. Many come from later generations of builders.
Inside some of them: jars of beer and loaves of bread, packed for the next world.
Same pay. Forever.
And think about where they are. Slaves don't get buried beside the king.

[S10 CLOSE]
So no, you weren't a slave. You weren't exactly a volunteer either.
You were a farm kid who got drafted, got fed, got hurt, got patched up, and got sent home.
And high inside the Great Pyramid, in sealed chambers above the king's burial room, there's red paint on the stone.
It isn't a prayer for the king. It's a team name.
Friends of Khufu.
The king wanted to be remembered forever. He was.
But so was the crew. They signed it.
Quest complete.
