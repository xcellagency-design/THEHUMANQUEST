# EP007 "Your Life Savings in 1923 Germany": fact reader and hostile editor report

Script read: `03_episodes/EP007/02_script/EP007_script_v2_final.txt` (90 rows, 0 to 89; 1,756 words, about 8:52 at 198 wpm before pauses and chimes).
Evidence: the notes in `01_facts/facts.json` only (47 rows, cited below as F0 to F46 in file order). No web, no outside knowledge.

Verdict: do not render as it stands. 6 SERIOUS, 25 MODERATE, 26 MINOR findings. All six serious ones have one-line fixes. All arithmetic was redone and is correct (list at the end of Part A); the problems are attribution, dating, sequence, and details that exist only in "the researcher's reading" and not in a returned fragment.

## PART A: fact check

### SERIOUS

**A1. Row 55** "In one Bavarian town, the archive records, the banks stopped handling any sum under fifty million marks at the end of September."
Note F27 holds a three-word fragment only: "unter 50 Millionen Mark". Who (the banks), when (end of September), what (stopped / announced) are all the researcher's reading. The source is a 2013 teaching sheet of the town archive, not an archival record, so "the archive records" overstates. The reading itself says the banks *announced* they would no longer process *transactions* under 50 million; the script says they *stopped handling any sum*, and "Yours is ten thousand" turns a transaction limit into a statement about a balance.
Fix: cut the row unless the sentence is re-read. If confirmed: "In one Bavarian town, the town archive says, the banks announced at the end of September: no transactions under fifty million marks. Yours is ten thousand."

**A2. Row 67** "Businessmen learn the rule... contract as many debts as possible. The best known, Hugo Stinnes, comes to hold one thousand five hundred and thirty-five companies."
F34 supports three separate things: Bresciani-Turroni's "rules of good management" sentence; LeMO's "Der Stinnes-Konzern umfaßt 1.535 Unternehmen" (year not returned); and "gehört er zu den Hauptprofiteuren der Inflation". Nothing in the note says Stinnes built the 1,535 companies by borrowing. Placing him as "the best known" of the businessmen who learnt the debt rule asserts exactly that. "Best known" is admitted in the note to be the script's word. "Comes to hold" implies growth over the period; the source says "comprises", undated. The note also records a conflicting count (NDB: more than 4,000 directly or indirectly).
Fix: "One economist wrote the rule down: contract as many debts as possible. And the German Historical Museum counts Hugo Stinnes among the inflation's main profiteers. His group comprised fifteen hundred and thirty-five companies."

**A3. Row 73** "No more new notes for the state."
Contradicted by its own note (F40): Sargent gives "the maximum amount that could be issued to the government, 1.2 billion marks" of Rentenmarks, i.e. the state did get new notes, from the new bank, up to a limit. The note also says Reichsbank circulation tripled by 31 December.
Fix: "The central bank stops cashing the government's bills. The printing press is closed to the state." Or: "No more paper marks on demand."

**A4. Row 85** "What did most for it, says the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, was the Great Depression: falling prices and mass unemployment. The opposite kind of disaster."
F43: the museum's words are "What most contributed to the Nazi Party's success was Germany's economic collapse during the Great Depression". "Falling prices and mass unemployment" is, by the note's own admission, "this script's gloss", built from a Delors Centre paper and an abstract. By ear the gloss is inside the attribution, so a named museum is credited with words it did not say, on the most sensitive passage in the script. "The opposite kind of disaster" is the script's claim and is half contradicted by row 71 (nearly one union member in four out of work in November 1923; F38 has 28.2% in December): mass unemployment was not the opposite of 1923.
Fix: "The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum says what did most for the Nazi Party was Germany's economic collapse in the Great Depression. And that was a time of falling prices. The opposite problem."

**A5. Row 38** "The September before, the reporter Ernest Hemingway crossed into the German town of Kehl..."
F17 dates the article 19 September 1922 and warns "a year before the peak". The last time markers heard are "In August" (34), "By autumn" (35) and "October" (36). From October, "the September before" is heard as last month, September 1923. That mis-dates the anecdote into the peak. Also: the date is the article's publication date, not the crossing; the cents are Canadian (omitted); and "If you have foreign money, you're rich" is stated in the present of 1923 on the strength of one 1922 border-town anecdote, while the same note cautions that Berlin in February 1923 was dear for many goods and that foreign buying peaked in late 1922.
Fix: "Foreign money goes a long way. A year earlier, in September nineteen twenty-two, the reporter Ernest Hemingway wrote that he changed ten francs, about ninety cents, at the border town of Kehl. It bought a day of heavy spending for two."

**A6. Rows 44 to 48: order of events.** 27 October (tram, 44), then Betty Scholem's letter of 15 October (45) with no date, then "Eight days later" (46, = 23 October), then the ambassador's sum of 21 October (48) with "now".
Heard in order, the letter is on or after 27 October and "eight days later" lands in November. F23 and F24 date them 15, 23 and 21 October. The interval itself (15 to 23 = eight days) is right; the anchor is wrong.
Fix: reorder to 45, 46, 47 (open with "Mid-October. A printer's wife..."), then 48, then 44 as the closing figure ("By the twenty-seventh a tram ride..."), or give row 45 its date.

### MODERATE

**A7. Row 1** "the price is going up while you wait."
F45 says in terms: "no source says a bakery changed its price while a particular queue waited." Support is for rises within a day (Price, 18 Oct: ten o'clock versus five o'clock; Kroner, 26 Aug: yesterday versus today; Ullrich "stündlich", undated in the note). Following "In January..." and "It's nineteen twenty-three", the line applies it to the whole year.
Fix: "It's nineteen twenty-three. By autumn the price at five o'clock is not the price at ten."

**A8. Row 3** "The bread prices are real. Germany's own statistics office printed them."
True only of the monthly Berlin averages (F0). Not true of Betty Scholem's loaf prices (45, 46). And the note says the table was read as OCR with units "partly inferred" from August on; June and July rest on OCR alone (F1, qualified).
Fix: "The monthly bread prices are real. Germany's own statistics office printed them." (and confirm on the scan, as the note orders).

**A9. Row 7** (framing row) "It's the only one anybody wins."
A claim in a framing row, and contradicted inside the script: quest one is "complete" (32), quest two "call it complete" (50), and row 38 says people with foreign money are rich. No note supports "only".
Fix: "There's a fourth quest hidden at the end. It's the one you didn't know you were playing."

**A10. Row 9** "Germany paid for the war by borrowing, not by taxing. Then came the reparations bill. The state covered the gap with new banknotes."
(a) LeMO's fragment is "vor allem durch Anleihen" (mainly); the script is absolute. (b) The Bundestag fragment begins "die dafür nötigen Mittel" and the note does not show what "dafür" refers to; the note says "the gap" is shorthand for the budget deficit. The order of the three sentences makes reparations the cause of the printing, which no fragment states. "The gap" also has no antecedent for a listener.
Fix: "Germany paid for the war mostly by borrowing, not by taxing. Then came the reparations bill. And the state found the money it needed by printing more of its own."

**A11. Row 18** "Many are still paid by the week."
F8's evidence is two instances (Kroner, Betty Scholem) and Kuczynski's "twice a week, and even oftener", which is not weekly. "Many" is an inference.
Fix: "Not everyone. Some are still paid by the week."

**A12. Row 20** "A British newspaper printed that early this year..."
F9 is qualified: the Guardian article was not opened; this is Econlib (2023) quoting it. The script states it as plain fact.
Fix: "It is quoted from a British newspaper early this year, as a story going round Berlin." Or cut (see Part B).

**A13. Row 27** "In early August the wife of the diarist Victor Klemperer orders a coffee in a station waiting room."
F12: "the date (entry of 2/3 August 1923) and 'station' come from the researcher's reading"; Ullrich's own words are "in einem Wartesaal" on the journey home from a Baltic holiday. Neither "station" nor "early August" is in a fragment, and a diary entry of 2/3 August about a journey home does not date the coffee to August. It is also told as an event, when it is a diary entry quoted by a historian.
Fix: "That summer Victor Klemperer notes in his diary what happened to his wife on the way home from holiday. In a waiting room she orders a coffee. The board says six thousand marks."

**A14. Row 31** "Bread averages sixty-nine thousand marks a kilo that month. Your savings book now buys a seventh of a kilo."
"That month" hangs on the unverified date in A13. And "now" is wrong within the month: a coffee at 6,000 marks (and, F22, a tram fare of 10,000 on 1 August) sits at the start of a month whose average bread price is 69,000. At the moment of the coffee the book bought more than a seventh of a kilo.
Fix: "Over August, bread averages sixty-nine thousand marks a kilo. At that price your savings book buys a seventh of a kilo."

**A15. Row 34** "the president of the central bank announces that it is issuing twenty trillion marks of new money a day."
F13 is qualified: an English rendering in a popular history (Fergusson), no German original opened, the body addressed not settled. Told as plain fact. The conversion (20,000 milliard = 20 trillion; 46,000 = 46 trillion) is right.
Fix: "In August, by one account, the president of the central bank tells a state council it is issuing twenty trillion marks of new money a day. Next week, he says, forty-six."

**A16. Row 36** "A press photograph from October shows people writing on the back of a one-million-mark note. A new notepad would cost billions."
F15 is qualified. (a) "Press photograph" is unconfirmed for this image (the note says confirm first). (b) The note's evidence is the caption, not the picture: "Findige Leute benutzen die Rückseite... zum Schreiben"; whether people are in the photograph is not known. (c) "A new notepad would cost billions" is the agency caption's claim, given here as the narrator's fact.
Fix: "The caption of a photograph from October says resourceful people are writing on the back of the one-million-mark note. A new pad, it says, would cost billions."

**A17. Row 37** "Everyone watches the dollar. In Berlin the artist Käthe Kollwitz writes down what a child on the tram asked, looking at the telegraph wires."
F16: only "Mother, is that the dollar?" is a returned fragment. The telegraph wires are "from the researcher's reading (not a returned fragment)"; the tram and "In Berlin" are in no fragment either. The claim dates it November 1923, but the row sits before "October" (43), so position dates it earlier. "Everyone" rests on three anecdotes.
Fix (after re-reading the page): "People watch the dollar. That November the artist Käthe Kollwitz writes down a child's question, looking up at the telegraph wires. Mother, is that the dollar?" and move it to the November block, or drop the month.

**A18. Row 40** "The central bank's own history says..."

**A19. Row 48** "The British ambassador does a sum in his diary. A pound now costs sixty billion marks."

**A20. Row 54** "In September, at one and a half million marks a kilo: seven grams."

## PART B: hostile editor

**First three rows.** Pass. Row 0 puts me in a queue with a bag and gives 250 and 201 billion inside about ten seconds; row 2 states my stake in numbers. Two reservations: row 0 gives two dates before I know what day I am standing in; and row 3's disclaimer arrives at 0:22, before the hook has set.
