### What the numbers say about publishing volume

The only channel in this niche I found posting near your twice-daily target is Explain In Paint. It publishes about 6.9 videos a week in the same “ancient humans” lane and has a recent median of 6.8K views per video, against roughly 314K for Ink Explainer at 0.8 a week ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/channel/explaininpaint1), 25 Oct 2026). Primate Economics goes the other way: 0.3 videos a week, 574K subscribers and about 1.1M views per video ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/channel/primateeconomics), 6 Sep 2026). In this sample, more uploads go with fewer views per upload. That is a correlation across five channels, not proof, but it is the only evidence available and it points against launching at two a day.

One more finding matters. Ink Explainer is not a lone creator. A growth agency published a [case study](https://ytwealthmedia.com/case-studies/ink-explainer/) claiming it designed the format and pipeline and has already cloned it to a sister channel. You are competing with a production system, so being one more copy of it is the weakest position available.

### Lessons to take

- **One question, one promise.** Every top title is a single plain question or a single promise of completeness.
- **Second person works across niches.** “Your life as” and “what did they actually do” appear in the top five of two different channels.
- **Familiar subject, unfamiliar seat.** The German soldier at D-Day, the ordinary human in the Stone Age, the five-year-old learning about 2008.
- **Evergreen only.** None of the three depends on news.
- **Myth correction in the middle.** It gives viewers a reason to stay past the hook.

### Gaps they leave open

- **Nobody joins the lanes.** No channel here applies the second-person life story to money, or to events after the Stone Age and outside the World Wars.
- **The “ordinary person” seat is nearly empty for famous monuments and crashes.** One small channel, Footnote People, describes itself as fact-checked animated history from ordinary viewpoints, posting three times a week. I could not get its numbers. It is the closest direct competitor to watch.
- **Sources on screen.** Ink Explainer links studies in descriptions. None is described as showing its evidence inside the video.
- **Motion.** The format leader uses no animation at all, so even light movement is a visible upgrade.
- **A recurring character.** None of the three has a named lead that viewers could recognise on a thumbnail.

### A. Character design (observed)

- **Proportions:** round white head about a third of standing height, so figures are roughly three heads tall. Thick, even black outline.
- **Face:** two dot eyes and a one-stroke mouth, with eyebrow strokes for emotion. No nose, no ears.
- **Body:** the modern figure is bare lines. Prehistoric figures get a filled brown hide tunic and a shaggy hair mass. Hands are stubs, with fingers only in close-ups.
- **Telling people apart:** hair shape and colour do all the work. Brown shaggy hair is the protagonist, a grey bun is the grandmother, long dark hair is the mother. A green headband and a red bead necklace mark two adults.
- **Expressions:** about eight states built from eyebrow angle and mouth curve (neutral, smile, open joy, worry, pain, smug, shock, asleep).
- **Mouth and eye movement:** none. There is no lip sync anywhere.
- **Walking and running:** no cycles. Running is one mid-stride pose with short speed lines.
- **Figures per image:** usually one to three, up to about twelve in crowd stills.
- **Interaction:** shown by pose only, such as a baby held, a spear raised or food handed over.
- **Consistency:** roles stay consistent but the model drifts. Head size, line weight and face details change from still to still, and the couch figure gains a beard and glasses as it ages.

### B. Animation technique

**Observed:** flat 2D stills cut on the narration. Movement is implied by two to four stills of the same scene with a changed pose. There is no rigging, no camera and no layered compositing in the story. The sponsor read uses a simple presenter puppet beside screen recordings.

**Likely, but an assumption:** the stills are generated in bulk from a style reference and laid on the voiceover in an editor. The evidence is the volume (375 unique drawings), the drift between stills and the uniform rendering. The file cannot prove it.

### C. Scene composition

- **Three picture types.** Full scenes with a background (savannah camp, hut interior, night fire) are about 60%. White-card diagrams are next: a 0–70 lifespan bar, maps with a red pin, an iceberg, crossed-out icons. Text cards hold two or three hand-lettered words with a small icon in the top-right corner.
- **Recurring anchors.** A modern figure on a grey couch stands for the viewer and returns about a dozen times. A winding path with signposts returns about eight times to mark life-stage “checkpoints”. A split screen with a dashed divider compares then and now.
- **Camera.** Eye level and medium-wide. A close-up is simply a new still.
- **Palette (sampled from 121 stills).** White #FEFEFE 24%, sky blue #B1D6E6 16%, ochre #B88C5E 14%, hide brown #7D6348 9%, sand #D8CAB8 9%, dark brown #4E433A 8%, straw #DDB674 7%, fire orange #B66040 4%, ink #161314 4%.
- **Lighting.** Flat. Night is a navy sky with an orange fire glow.
- **Text.** All-caps hand-lettered marker in black, with red for emphasis. Text sits on white cards and almost never inside a scene.

### D. Voice and narration

- **Measured:** adult male register, about 228 words per minute, almost no pauses, very even loudness. Sentences average about ten words.
- **Not confirmed:** accent, and whether the voice is human or synthetic.
- **Style from the transcript:** second person throughout, plain vocabulary, dry asides, one direct question to the viewer at 5:15. Studies are told as small stories about what scientists did, not as citations.
- **Sync:** each sentence gets one or two pictures, and the picture changes on the noun it illustrates.
- **For our channel:** keep the second person and the short sentences. The 130–160 words per minute in your brief would stretch this same script to 18–22 minutes, so I recommend 180–200 with pauses trimmed. That figure is a hypothesis to test, since the reference succeeds at 228.

### E. Music and sound

- **Measured:** a continuous low bed sits about 23 dB under the voice and never swells. There is no music-only moment and no silence longer than 1.3 s.
- **Not confirmed:** genre, and whether individual effects (whooshes, impacts) exist. If they do, they are mixed too low to measure.
- **Reading:** sound is deliberately out of the way. The voice is the soundtrack.

### F. Timing and story structure

| Time | Beat | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:13 | Cold open | A claim about the viewer’s own birth, then the stakes. No greeting, no title card |
| 0:13–0:26 | Premise | Puts “you” 50,000 years back with the same body and brain |
| 0:26–0:36 | Loop 1 | Teases what the “died at 30” number hides |
| 0:36–0:48 | Loop 2 | Promises a reveal tied to the viewer’s exact current age |
| 0:48–0:52 | Sponsor tag | Three seconds, then straight back |
| 0:52–3:21 | Birth and infancy | Plants a line that is called back at the end. First “checkpoint” passed |
| 3:22–4:41 | Sponsor read | 80 s, bridged by theme (staying hidden meant staying safe) |
| 4:43–6:40 | Childhood | Pays off loop 1 at 6:03, then opens a new tease |
| 6:40–9:15 | Teens to twenties | Second myth corrected. First third of the age promise paid at 8:01 |
| 9:15–10:17 | Thirties | Second third paid at 9:47 |
| 10:17–11:45 | Forties onward | Final third paid at 11:24 |
| 11:45–12:50 | Death and close | Oldest known burial, the planted callback, and the opening line repeated with a new meaning |

Three devices are worth learning from. The age promise is paid in three instalments, so viewers aged 20, 33 and 55 each get a line aimed at them. The checkpoints give a sense of progress every two to three minutes. A modern comparison lands every 40–60 seconds, which keeps ancient material personal.

### The lead character: Sid

Sid is short for side quest, and stands for “you” in every episode. The design deliberately avoids the round-headed stick figure every channel in this niche uses.

- **Head:** a rounded square, slightly wider than tall, Paper fill, with one curled tuft of hair on top.
- **Face:** two short vertical dashes for eyes and a single mouth stroke. Eyebrows appear only for strong emotion.
- **Body:** a Marigold capsule torso about 1.2 heads tall, Ink line limbs, mitten hands, small boot blobs. Total height is 3.5 heads.
- **Line:** Ink, slightly wobbly, constant weight of about 6 pixels at 1080p.
- **Era costume:** one garment drawn over the torso per episode, such as a linen kilt, a toga or a 1920s suit. The tuft and a Marigold band always stay visible.
- **Quest marker:** the teal diamond appears over Sid’s head when an objective starts or completes.
- **Everyone else:** same head shape, grey-beige torsos and no tuft, so Sid is findable in any crowd in under a second.

**Channel description (draft).** You are dropped into the past with no money, no power and a short list of things to survive. The Human Side Quest tells history, money and science from the point of view of the ordinary person standing in the middle of it. Every episode is researched from published studies, primary documents and museum sources, linked below each video. New episodes every week.

## G. Production and automation stack

Build the system to handle two videos a day, run it at three a week, and raise the pace only when quality holds. Every public upload waits for your approval.

### Why not two a day from day one

- **The evidence.** Section A: the three channels you want to compete with post 0.4–0.8 times a week. The one near-daily channel in the niche has a median of 6.8K views.
- **The policy.** On 15 July 2025 YouTube updated its Partner Program rules to better identify mass-produced and repetitious content, now called “inauthentic content”. YouTube said AI tools are still allowed and such channels stay eligible ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/youtube-clarifies-monetization-update-inauthentic-repeated-content/752892/), [eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/youtube-responds-to-ai-concerns/)). A new channel posting 60 templated AI videos a month is the pattern that rule describes.
- **The hours.** My estimate is about 12 hands-on hours per episode at this quality bar (table below). Two a day is roughly 24 hours of skilled work every day, so it needs a team of three or four, not more automation.

**Gates I propose.** Move to daily after 13 episodes if three things hold for three straight weeks: nine in ten episodes pass quality control first time, average percentage viewed has not fallen against the first six episodes, and six finished episodes sit in reserve. Move to two a day after 30 days at daily on the same terms, with a second human reviewer in place. These thresholds are my proposal, not an industry standard.

### Daily workflow

1. **Discover (automated, 06:00).** Pull competitor uploads and outliers, score new ideas with the section D rubric, and check each against the topic log for duplicates.
2. **Select (human, 5 minutes).** Approve or swap the top two ideas.
3. **Verify (automated draft, human check).** Build a fact table with one opened source per claim. Any claim with no source is cut.
4. **Script (automated draft, human edit).** Second person, quest log, word count sized to the runtime.
5. **Approve script (human).** First hard checkpoint. Nothing is generated before this.
6. **Voice.** Generate by section, listen once, re-take flubs.
7. **Shot list.** Split the voice track into 1.5–2.5 second beats and write one image line per beat.
8. **Stills.** Generate with the locked prefix and reference sheet. Auto-reject frames with text or the wrong palette. A human picks the rest.
9. **Hero motion.** Animate the 8–12 marked shots.
10. **Assemble.** Lay stills on the voice, add build-ons, cards, bed and effects, and normalise loudness.
11. **Package.** Three thumbnails, three titles, description, tags, pinned comment.
12. **Quality control (human).** Full watch at normal speed against the checklist in section I. A fail goes back to the failed step and the video stays in draft.
13. **Upload as private** through the API with the scheduled time attached.
14. **Approve publication (human).** Second hard checkpoint. You confirm the schedule in YouTube Studio or with one click in the automation.
15. **Record.** Log video ID, title and thumbnail version, then pull performance at 24 hours, 48 hours, 7 days and 28 days.

### YouTube connection

I do not have access to your YouTube account and have not uploaded anything.

- **Project.** Create a Google Cloud project, enable YouTube Data API v3 and YouTube Analytics API, and configure the OAuth consent screen.
- **Permissions.** Uploading needs the `youtube.upload` scope. Setting thumbnails and editing metadata needs `youtube` or `youtube.force-ssl`. Reading analytics needs `yt-analytics.readonly`.
- **Quota.** Google’s [reference page](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/docs/videos/insert) currently lists an upload as 1 unit from a separate video-uploads allowance of 100 calls a day, far above two a day. - **Who signs in.** You authorise once with the channel’s Google account. The automation stores a refresh token and nothing else.
- **Disclosure.** YouTube asks creators to label realistic altered or synthetic content. Stylised cartoons were exempt when I last read the rule, but I did not re-check it today, so read the current help page before the first upload.

### Failure handling

- **Failed generation.** Retry twice with a new seed, then flag the shot for a human. Never ship a substitute frame silently.
- **Off-model character.** Compare each still to the reference sheet. Mismatched head shape, missing tuft or wrong torso colour means a re-roll.
- **Text inside an image.** Automatic reject.
- **Duplicate topic.** Compare the new title and central question with the topic log, and block anything that overlaps a published or scheduled episode.
- **Copyright.** No competitor frames, no real logos, no music without a certificate on file. Compare the script with source texts and rewrite any sentence that tracks a source too closely.
- **Missed slot.** If quality control is not passed two hours before the slot, the slot is skipped. Nothing unfinished is published.

Track views, impressions, click-through rate, average view duration, average percentage viewed, the retention curve, subscribers gained, likes, comments, shares, returning viewers, traffic sources and revenue for each video at 24 hours, 48 hours, 7 days and 28 days. What one thing changes next week? A topic is repeated only when the reason it worked can be stated in a sentence.

### 3. Script v1 (narration)

**S1. Cold open (0:00–0:23)**

You were told slaves built the pyramids. Whips. Chains. A hundred thousand prisoners in the sand.

That picture comes from a Greek writer who showed up two thousand years late.

The people who actually built it left a diary, a payroll, and their own skeletons.

And their story is stranger. They were paid in beer. They couldn’t say no. And one crew called itself the Drunkards.

So let’s make you one of them.

**S2. Premise (0:23–0:41)**

It’s about 2560 BC. You’re seventeen. You farm barley in a village on the Nile.

You are made up. Everything that happens to you is in the evidence.

Here’s your quest log. One: get to Giza. Two: earn your bread. Three: get home in one piece.

There’s a fourth quest hidden at the end. Almost nobody sees it coming.

**S3. Quest one: the draft (0:41–1:18)**

Every summer the Nile floods your fields. For weeks, there’s nothing to farm.

That’s when the king’s men arrive. They don’t want your grain. They want you.

Historians still argue about how much choice you had.

Zahi Hawass, Egypt’s best-known archaeologist, calls men like you conscripts.

Mark Lehner, who dug up your future home, calls it obligatory labor. A tax you pay with your back.

He compares it to a barn-raising. Everyone is expected to show up. Not everyone is thrilled.

So you’re not a slave. Nobody owns you. But “no” isn’t on the menu either.

You climb into a boat with the other boys from your village, and you sail for Giza.

Quest one, complete.

**S4. The city (1:18–2:00)**

You expect a camp. You get a city.

Archaeologists call it the Lost City of the Pyramid Builders. The crown built it for exactly one job.

There are royal grain silos. There are bakeries by the dozen.

You sleep in a long hall, shoulder to shoulder, with forty or fifty other men.

About twenty thousand people may live here, counting the bakers, the brewers and the scribes.

Not a hundred thousand. And only about half of them ever touch a stone.

Then you get your team. Crews had names, and they painted them on the blocks.

One was the Friends of Khufu. A few kings later, another was the Drunkards of Menkaure.

Picture a company softball team. Except the season lasts for months, and the ball weighs two and a half tons.

**S5. Quest two: the job (2:00–2:48)**

Here’s the job. The Great Pyramid needs about 2.3 million blocks.

If the work took around twenty-three years, that’s roughly 270 blocks a day.

One block every two or three minutes. All day. For two decades.

A typical block weighs about as much as a pickup truck.

When it’s finished it will stand 146 metres tall. Nothing humans build will beat it for nearly four thousand years.

And you have rope, a wooden sledge, and your crew.

Plus one trick. Somebody walks in front and pours water on the sand.

It looks like a ritual. It’s physics.

In 2014, physicists in Amsterdam tested it. Damp sand is about twice as stiff as dry sand.

The sledge stops digging in, and the force you need drops by half.

A later Egyptian tomb painting shows exactly that. A man at the front of a sledge, pouring water.

Half the effort. Still a truck.

**S6. The diary (2:48–3:29)**

The best stone doesn’t come from here. The white outer blocks come from Tura, across the river.

We know because one of your coworkers kept a logbook.

His name was Merer. He ran a boat crew of about forty men.

In 2013 his diary turned up at an ancient harbour on the Red Sea. It’s the oldest inscribed papyrus ever found.

And it is gloriously boring. Load stone. Sail to the pyramid. Sleep there. Sail back. Repeat.

Two or three round trips every ten days. About two hundred blocks a month.

He never once says the pyramid is amazing. He calls it by its name, Horizon of Khufu, and goes back for more rocks.

It’s a timesheet. It just happens to be four and a half thousand years old.

**S7. Payday (3:29–4:04)**

So what does all this pay? Not coins. Coins won’t exist for almost two thousand years.

You’re paid in food. The standard ration for a laborer in this era was ten loaves of bread and a measure of beer. Per day.

Beer here is a staple food, not a party.

Bread moulds are the most common pottery in the whole city. Beer jars come second.

And there’s meat. Hawass estimates twenty-one cattle and twenty-three sheep arrived every single day.

Lehner thinks most of it was served at big feasts.

Either way, you’re eating beef. For a farm kid, that’s a feast.

Quest two, complete. You earned your bread. Literally.

**S8. Quest three: the body (4:04–4:49)**

Quest three is the hard one. Get home in one piece.

Because the builders’ bones were found too. And bones keep receipts.

Worn-down spines. Worn-out joints. Arms and legs that had snapped.

But here’s the twist. Most of those breaks had healed straight.

Somebody set them with a splint.

A few skeletons even show amputations the patient survived.

So when a block rolls onto your leg, and one day something will, you aren’t left in the sand.

Someone carries you out. Someone sets the bone. And you keep getting your bread.

Four and a half thousand years ago, you had workplace healthcare.

It still wasn’t a long life. Most of the people in that cemetery died in their thirties.

But if your months are up and your legs still work, you sail home. A new crew takes your bunk.

Quest three, complete. Barely.

**S9. Hidden quest (4:49–5:15)**

Now the hidden one.

The pyramid is a machine for one man’s afterlife. Yours isn’t in the budget.

But in 1990, a cemetery turned up in the shadow of the pyramids. Small, modest tombs. The builders’ own.

Not every grave belongs to Khufu’s crews. Many come from later generations of builders.

Inside some of them: jars of beer and loaves of bread, packed for the next world.

Same pay. Forever.

And think about where they are. Slaves don’t get buried beside the king.

**S10. Close (5:15–5:42)**

So no, you weren’t a slave. You weren’t exactly a volunteer either.

You were a farm kid who got drafted, got fed, got hurt, got patched up, and got sent home.

And high inside the Great Pyramid, in sealed chambers above the king’s burial room, there’s red paint on the stone.

It isn’t a prayer for the king. It’s a team name.

Friends of Khufu.

The king wanted to be remembered forever. He was.

But so was the crew. They signed it.

Quest complete.

### 7. Voice and sound
